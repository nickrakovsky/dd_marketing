A truck is waiting while someone chases missing paperwork. Everyone knows the load is delayed. What nobody has checked is how close it is to running past its agreed free time.

DataDocks creator and CEO Nick Rakovsky explains why tracking shipments is only the start. The team also needs to know when a delay is becoming urgent, while someone can still do something about it.

Watch Nick walk through the practical steps below (9:32), from knowing what is in the yard to setting up warnings that reach the people responsible.

## Decide what should trigger a response

Nick starts with visibility: track every shipment and know which trailers and containers are on site. Without that information, an urgent load can sit unnoticed while the team works through whatever arrived next.

Then make the information useful during the shift. For a waiting load, the team needs to know what is blocking progress, who can resolve it, and when the situation needs escalating. Missing documents call for someone who can supply them. Unclear handling instructions need an answer from the customer. A warning should reach the person able to move that particular load forward.

## Leave time between the warning and the threshold

Knowing that a load has already exceeded its free time helps explain a bill. An earlier warning gives the team a chance to investigate before that point.

For illustration, suppose an appointment's agreed clock starts at 09:00 and allows two hours before waiting charges can begin. Compare a warning at 11:00 with one at 10:30. The earlier warning creates a 30-minute window to act. It does not unload the truck or resolve the missing paperwork by itself.

Choose the warning time around the work someone needs to do after receiving it. If a coordinator has to contact a customer and get a document corrected, a notification at the threshold leaves little room to respond. Agree who receives the warning and what they should do next.

The same preparation applies when a 3PL handles the freight. Nick recommends supplying arrival details and instructions in advance, and understanding the waiting fees before a load gets held up.
