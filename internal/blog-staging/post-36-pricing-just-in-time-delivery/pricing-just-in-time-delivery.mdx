Suppose a manufacturer asks us to quote on getting parts to their plant by noon. Our regular carrier can make the run and we know the rate. Before we send the quote, I’d want to know what happens at that plant if our truck misses pickup. Can the parts go a little later? Or is there a crew waiting on them to start the next job?

When I was running facilities, we had some flexibility because several customers bought the same product. If we were going to come up short on a contract, we could redirect a load we’d intended for another buyer. We still had that other order to cover, but moving the load bought us time to work through the supply problem. We could do all of that, have the right product ready at the dock, and still miss the delivery if the truck didn’t show.

## Find out how much time the customer has

This is where I want to hear from the salesperson who talks to the buyer. One customer might be able to move the job to the afternoon. Another may have done that for us twice already, and the buyer has made it clear they can’t do it again. Both might have a receiving door open at two o’clock. Only one can wait that long for the parts.

That’s when I’d get operations involved in the quote. If we’re willing to pay twice the usual rate to get this load there, who can actually pick it up, and when will it reach the customer? There’s a difference between a carrier we can call that morning and one that will set capacity aside for us. The second may cost us money even on days when the regular truck shows up.

At two misses, booking on the spot and reserving capacity each add $320 across ten deliveries. That gives sales $32 in extra freight per delivery to allow for in the quote. Now move the deadline to noon. The spot truck arrives at 13:00. We could negotiate a better rate on that truck and we’d still be an hour late. In this example, keeping the noon promise means reserving capacity before we know which pickup will need it.

## Take the recovery cost back to the quote

For a real account, I’d bring the pickup history on that lane and the backup carrier’s terms into the conversation with sales. Those give us a basis for deciding how much room to leave in the margin. Leadership might decide the account is worth absorbing some of that cost. We might quote more for noon, or offer an afternoon window the customer can use.

I’d want that settled while the buyer can still choose. Once the plant has planned its run around our parts, the person on the dock has very little say in what service we’ve sold.
