When we used staffing services for unloading trailers and other logistics work, we tried to arrange the relationship so we could hire people after an agreed period. That gave us a way to meet people through the work itself. When someone did a good job and we wanted to keep them, there was a route to bringing them onto our team.

I saw that as a net positive. It helped us stay nimble, and the service didn't create a huge overhead for us at the time. But it came with uneven results. Some people didn't show up or weren't suitable for the work. Others did the job adequately without being people we wanted to hire permanently. The people we kept were the ones we particularly valued.

That distinction matters. Filling a position for a shift and finding someone you want on the team are related outcomes, but they aren't interchangeable. An agency worker can do useful work without becoming a permanent hire. Equally, finding a good person only helps build the team if you have a practical way to keep them and work for them to do.

## What changes when the same people return?

We also had to find staffing services that worked for us. Some couldn't reliably supply people or communicate well. Preparation varied too. With some services, we had to take on training we expected to have been done; the better ones sent people ready for the work.

That makes the cost of repeated introductions worth examining. Even when every position is filled, a changing group can require more first-day instruction and attention from the people already there. The amount depends on the job and how well the agency prepares its workers. Simply counting heads won't tell you what that asks of your existing team.

## Make the route to staying part of the arrangement

For me, the practical value was being able to act when we found people we wanted to keep. That starts with understanding the agency's hiring arrangement, rather than discovering the conditions after someone has become valuable to the operation. It also means being clear about the work available and whether the person wants to join the team.

I'd assess the service on both time horizons. Is it providing reliable, prepared people for the work we need done now? And, where we need permanent staff, is it helping us find people we want to retain? A service can perform well on one and poorly on the other.

Our experience was mixed, but worthwhile. The opportunity was to keep learning which people and which providers worked for us, then turn the strongest working relationships into something more stable when the operation could support it.
