title: "What to Account for Before Bringing Truck Freight In-House"
author: "Nick Rakovsky"

When I look at bringing truck freight in-house, the capital cost is one of the first things I consider. Then I want to know who is going to manage it. Buying or assigning the equipment gets you part of the way. Someone also has to oversee the work, deal with repairs, troubleshoot problems and answer the call when the run isn't going to happen as planned.

I've been involved in bringing some truck transportation in-house. It was a little cheaper, and it gave us more headaches. We didn't have much flexibility when the driver was out, the truck was broken or the trailer we used for that particular lane was unavailable. The saving was there. So was the responsibility for finding a way to move the freight anyway.

That experience changed how I look at the comparison. Alongside the expected running cost, I want to understand what it takes to keep the service going when one part of the plan falls over.

A regular lane can make the work look predictable. You know where the truck goes and what it needs to move. But a spare vehicle only helps if someone can drive it, and another driver only helps if suitable equipment is available. Those resources also have to be available for this run, rather than already committed somewhere else.

In that example, borrowing a vehicle can protect the morning commitment while delaying the next one. A dedicated spare avoids that conflict, but it is capacity the business has to provide and maintain. Either way, getting the load moving still leaves someone responsible for the broken vehicle. I want to see those consequences alongside the expected saving before deciding the work belongs in-house.

In the truck transportation arrangement I described, it probably would have been easier to have a third party responsible for providing the service each day. We could tell them what we needed and make it their job to resolve an unavailable driver or truck. That wouldn't remove the need to manage the relationship. It would change which problems our own team had to solve.

This is why an apparent saving doesn't settle the decision for me. I want to put the management work beside it: maintenance, repairs, cover, HR issues and the interruptions that land on people already running the operation. Even a saving that looks substantial as a percentage may not contribute enough to the business to justify taking all of that on.

Before making the move, I'd also use the current provider as a chance to learn. Pay attention to what happens when something goes wrong, including the things they handle badly. Document how the work gets done and what your team would need to take over. If there's a smaller version you can test, use it to find out whether the arrangement works for you.

You may find you have the people, equipment and cover to make in-house freight worthwhile. You may also decide that keeping it outsourced makes sense for the foreseeable future. Either way, the comparison should include the service you need to keep delivering and the work someone must own to make that happen.
