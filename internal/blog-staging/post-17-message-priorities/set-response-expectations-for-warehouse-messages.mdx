An email asks whether a load has finished. A text asks about tomorrow's instructions. Meanwhile, a loading team needs a decision to continue. If all three arrive with the same expectation of an instant reply, the supervisor has to keep stopping to work out what matters first.

DataDocks creator and CEO Nick Rakovsky describes how adding emails and group messages can create another communication problem: supervisors feel they have to answer everything straight away. Agreeing how quickly different issues need a response helps the team use that information during the shift.

Watch Nick explain the communication mistakes he sees in warehouse operations below (15:29), from unclear shift plans to reporting demands that keep supervisors away from the floor.

## Make the expected response clear

Nick recommends being explicit about how serious an issue is, how the team should react, and how quickly someone should report back. Otherwise, each person has to guess what the sender expects.

For a recurring request, agree who needs to respond, what decision or action is needed, and when the answer is required. A question holding up an unload needs to reach someone who can make that decision. A routine completion update needs a reliable place where the team can find it.

Review who receives each message as well. Copying every supervisor into the same conversation can leave several people checking it while responsibility for the answer stays unclear.

## Give each update a place and a time

Suppose Shipping needs to decide what happens next with a load. Compare notifying the whole group with notifying the person responsible. The shared load record remains available to every team in both cases.

These are example agreements. Set your own around the work being held up and the people available to act. Make sure the sender knows how to reach the responsible person when a decision is needed now, and where to leave an update that can be reviewed later.

## Check the burden with your supervisors

Nick recommends asking supervisors how much time reporting takes and whether it keeps them from seeing what is happening on the floor.

Start with one message chain that repeatedly interrupts a shift. Ask which updates require a reply, who uses the information, and whether the same details are being entered elsewhere. Agree a change, then check with the team whether urgent questions are reaching the right person and routine updates remain easy to find.
