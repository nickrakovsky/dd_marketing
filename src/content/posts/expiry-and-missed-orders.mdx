If your food business is weighing proposals for automation, better planning, more storage and more people, each proposal can sound useful. The harder question is which one will help when food is still expiring and customer orders are being missed.

I would start by working backward from those failures. What happened to the order we could not get out? What food expired, and why did we have it? Then I would compare what we expect an improvement to do with what it costs and the money we have available. A convincing proposal needs to address something the operation is actually lacking.

## Work backward from the orders you missed

Take an order that was supposed to leave and ask what prevented it. Was the product ready? Did something happen that stopped us getting it out? Or did the team find out about the requirement so late that it had very little time to respond?

That last question matters. If communication is poor and operations keeps learning about orders at the last minute, we need to understand that before deciding what to buy. The missed shipment tells us something went wrong. It does not yet tell us whether the answer is equipment, people or earlier information.

I would also look at whether space is driving decisions that do not make much sense. Are we continually shipping goods out because we cannot keep them on the floor? What is that pressure making the operation do, and what is it costing us? If lack of space is part of the difficulty, it needs to be understood alongside the other reasons work is not getting done.

## Find out which food is expiring

I would ask the same kind of questions about the food we are writing off. Are ingredients expiring before we use them, or are we producing more finished goods than we need? Those possibilities take the conversation in different directions.

If it is ingredients, I would ask whether we can forecast our needs better. Sales should have an idea of what it expects to sell. I would hold sales accountable for bringing that picture into the discussion so operations has something useful to work with when planning what ingredients it needs.

That may create an opportunity to bring ingredients in closer to when they will be used. But first we need to understand what is happening now. Ingredients expiring is a reason to investigate the forecast and purchasing pattern; it does not establish that we have already found the cause.

If we are overproducing finished goods, the question becomes why we are making that amount. We should not assume the write-off started with ingredients when the problem may be further along in production.

Writing in <SmartLink href="https://www.ift.org/publications/food-technology-magazine/20262/april/reinventing-food-waste/" title="Reinventing Food Waste" snippet="Food Technology Magazine, Institute of Food Technologists · 2026" anchorText="Food Technology Magazine in April 2026" />, Lara Ramdin describes the task as “Pinpointing where loss occurs and what drives it”. Her discussion explains why an overall waste figure offers too little detail to decide where to act. For our operation, I would come back to the food itself: what expired, why was it there, and what would have needed to happen differently?

## Check whether the proposed improvement addresses the cause

A production line can run faster while the business continues missing orders. If the team still finds out about an order too late, extra speed has left that information problem in place. We could improve the line and still be asking why the next shipment did not go out.

That is why I would want to connect an equipment proposal to the difficulty we have identified. What work would it help us complete? Was our ability to do that work what held up the order? If it was, there may be a good reason to invest. If the missing piece was advance notice, we still need to deal with that.

The same reasoning applies to planning. Where ingredient purchases are out of step with what sales expects, better information may help us plan what we need. We should be able to explain how the proposed change would improve that situation before treating a new planning system as the answer.

A <SmartLink href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44196-026-01475-w" title="An Intelligent Decision System for Sustainable Food Production: Mitigating Waste via CVaR Stochastic Optimisation" snippet="International Journal of Computational Intelligence Systems · 2026" anchorText="July 2026 study by Salvia Hornos and colleagues" /> puts the timing question directly: “given fixed production volumes, when should they be scheduled?” Using historical data from a meat factory, the researchers modelled changes to production timing while keeping total output and production rates fixed. Their retrospective analysis examined production scheduling and exposure to products expiring. It illustrates why timing deserves attention alongside speed, without establishing what savings another factory would achieve.

For the decision in front of us, I would keep asking where the operation is falling short and whether the proposed improvement changes that condition. A faster line and earlier knowledge of demand solve different problems. We need to know which one we have, or whether both need attention.

## Compare the benefit with what the business must commit

Once we understand what a proposal could change, I would put the expected benefit beside the overall cost and our budget today. How much do we think we will get back from the smaller investment? What additional benefit would justify spending considerably more?

Opportunity cost matters here too. Money committed to one improvement affects what else we can afford to do. I would want to understand what we are getting for that commitment, especially if another problem still needs money and attention. The cheapest option only helps if it makes a useful difference to the operation.

We may need more than one change. If late information and limited production capacity are both contributing to missed orders, addressing only one may leave us with work we still cannot get out. That possibility belongs in the comparison when we discuss what each proposal is likely to achieve.

The decision should bring us back to the orders and food we investigated. We need to explain what the spending will change, how much benefit we expect and why that is worth the commitment. Before choosing the first investment, I would want a clear answer to one question: which part of the failure will this help us prevent?
