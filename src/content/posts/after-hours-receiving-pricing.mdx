At 4:50 p.m., dispatch calls with a new ETA for the last truck. It should be at the gate by 5:15. Receiving closes at five. The shift lead can keep a receiver and a forklift operator until 5:45, long enough to unload the pallets if the truck comes when promised. Dispatch wants a quick yes. The person who will see the extra charge on an invoice is the 3PL’s client.

What the client can buy at 4:50 is a commitment. Two people will be available to receive this load after the normal shift ends. A usable price gives the lead an amount to quote, tells the client what that amount covers and gives billing a way to recognize the service after the truck leaves.

## Define what the warehouse is selling after five

Start with the receiving hours and handling terms already agreed for the account. Is five o’clock the last check-in time, or does unloading have to finish by then? A truck booked for 4:30 that arrived on time and waited for the warehouse to clear a door poses a different commercial question from a carrier calling to request a later arrival. Write down which event makes a receipt eligible for an additional charge. The client should be able to tell from the terms how each case will be handled.

Crossdock 24/365, a Plainfield, Illinois operator, lists a $100 after-hours fee for the period from 5 p.m. to 8 a.m. Its customer FAQ expressly offers off-hours drop-offs and pickups for a fee.<Cite id="1" title="Cross Docking &amp; Storage Near You, Chicago, Il" citation="Crossdock 24/365, undated" /> A customer with a delayed truck can see that the service exists and what the operator lists for it. Your warehouse’s price will depend on the coverage you agree to provide.

## Cost the whole receipt before setting the supplement

Look up several recent receipts of the kind the client wants handled late. Follow each one from the time the crew had to be available through to the time its last member could leave. A receiver may finish the paperwork while the forklift operator is still putting pallets away. A guard may have a minimum call-out even for a short job. At a site already working a second shift, check which outbound work would lose an operator to the unload.

Jonas Olsson’s research at PostNord Logistics TPL combined warehouse records, time studies and observations to estimate the labor required by activities including unloading.<Cite id="2" title="Time-driven activity-based costing within third-party logistics" citation="Olsson, 2017" /> The same method works on a small sample of evening receipts. Match the load record to the people who handled it and use the rates your business actually pays for those hours.

For the 5:15 truck, the crew’s paid extension starts at five. Those 15 minutes before arrival belong in the quote. Include evening pay and payroll burden for both people, then check how much handling already appears on the client’s invoice.

## Choose a price the desk can quote

A fixed supplement is easy to authorize for a familiar load and a bounded extension. If the trailer turns out to be floor-loaded or dispatch revises its ETA again to 5:40, the original quote needs another look. Agree in advance what another 15 minutes costs and who can approve it before the crew commits to staying.

Check where costs jump before promising a fixed rate per quarter-hour. If the security contractor bills a second minimum at six, staying from 5:45 to 6:15 costs more than an extra half-hour of crew time.

An hourly rate with a minimum can work better when unload times vary widely. Specify whether the rate buys an entire crew or one employee-hour. A warehouse benchmarking report published by Armstrong & Associates in January 2026 lists separate standard and overtime labor charges in its U.S. contract warehouse pricing table.<Cite id="3" title="Even-Keeled: The Business of Warehousing in North America" citation="Armstrong &amp; Associates, 2026" /> Those are prices operators charge customers. Use site payroll and staffing requirements to build the cost underneath your own quote.

## Give receiving and billing the same agreement

On a shared appointment sheet, put the approved amount, coverage end time and authorizing client contact beside the load reference. Agree what happens if the carrier cancels after staff have committed to stay. At closeout, record arrival, completion, actual crew release and any approved extension. Billing can then match the supplement to the authorization, and operations can compare the price with what the receipt consumed. If two clients shared the same evening crew, allocate the common coverage cost across their work when reviewing profitability.

A recurring 5:15 arrival deserves a conversation about the daytime slot, too. LeanCor told *DC Velocity* how it worked with suppliers and truckers at one customer facility to spread inbound pallet volume across two shifts and reduce the need for overtime.<Cite id="4" title="Lean your warehouse workforce" citation="Gooley, 2013" /> Ask when the freight is ready upstream. A workable earlier slot gives this client a choice alongside the evening price.

If the client chooses an earlier window, the desk can reserve the final slot for other accounts. In DataDocks, <SmartLink href="https://docs.datadocks.com/locations/current/advanced/capacity-adjustments/" anchorText="a recurring capacity adjustment" title="Capacity Adjustments" snippet="DataDocks Documentation" /> can restrict a selected company’s last slot until a review date. Set that date for the next account review and bring the authorized evening receipts, amounts billed and crew hours. The next time dispatch calls at 4:50, the lead can offer a confirmed evening price or a daytime appointment the carrier can make.
