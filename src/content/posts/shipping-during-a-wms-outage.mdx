Put yourself at the shipping desk with a driver waiting to leave. You know which order is on the trailer. The customer changed it this morning, and you need to see the signed change before you can clear the load. The warehouse management system (WMS) has just gone down.

One of the first questions I’d ask is how long the shipping desk could be in that position. Can the IT consultant respond during this shift? If your own technician covers several warehouses, how long would it take them to get to this one? A pickup window can close while somebody who knows how to fix the problem is still on the road.

## What can the shipping desk establish on paper?

In this example, the site has a manual release procedure. If the supervisor has the signed change and can match it to the load, the truck can leave. If the only document at the desk is a list printed before the change, the supervisor has to hold the truck until someone can verify the release. Getting that signed change into the crew’s hands can make the difference for the driver waiting at the door.

Now put yourself on the shift that takes over. The WMS is back, the order still shows *Open*, and the customer wants to know where the freight is. If you find the signed release, you can tell the customer the load was cleared. To say it left, you need the dock’s record of what happened at pickup.

Keep the recovery time at 09:45 below. Give the crew the signed changes, then change how often the office gets a copy of the dock slips. Watch which pickups the next shift can account for.

Look at the last two pickups before 09:45. Order 484 stayed at the dock awaiting approval. Order 485 left. With slips sent hourly, the office has the 09:00 copy, and both pickups happened after that. With that copy in hand, the incoming supervisor has to trace both pickups. If the slips come over after each pickup, the supervisor knows to chase the release for 484 and enter the departure for 485. Booking another pickup for 485 would send a driver for freight that’s already gone.

That’s how I’d test a warehouse outage plan. Pick one outbound order, walk it through the site’s manual procedure, then give the paperwork to someone who wasn’t at the dock. Ask them what they would do with that open order. If they have to find the person who worked the door before they can answer the customer, you’ve found the part of the job that still needs a backup.
