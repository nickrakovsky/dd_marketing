The same forklift is back in service after another repair. Maintenance has the parts and labour recorded. The shipping team knows which loads fell behind while it was unavailable. When someone asks whether to keep repairing the machine, those two accounts need to come together.

DataDocks creator and CEO Nick Rakovsky describes how recurring equipment faults can become familiar enough that everyone works around them. A manager knows a machine keeps causing trouble, but may struggle to show what that trouble costs across the operation.

In the video below (11:21), Nick explains how maintenance records, operator feedback, and equipment planning can help reduce warehouse downtime.

## Connect each repair to what happened on the floor

Nick recommends bringing inspection and maintenance records together so repeated faults become easier to see. A series of small repairs can look different once the maintenance time, replacement parts, and disruption to loading are considered together.

Start with one machine that keeps returning to maintenance. Record the fault, the work done, and how long it was unavailable. Add any rental or other cover arranged, then ask the shift supervisor what happened to the work during that period. Could another machine handle it? Did loads wait, or did work carry into the next shift?

Keep those consequences specific. Two hours without a forklift does not automatically mean two hours of lost loading across the warehouse. Other equipment may absorb the work. During a busy period, the same interruption may leave a queue that outlasts the repair.

## Check how much capacity remains

Suppose three forklifts can each handle 10 pallets an hour. One becomes unavailable, leaving capacity for 20. At 20 pallets of incoming work an hour, the remaining machines can keep up. At 25, unfinished work accumulates.

The example explains why a maintenance log needs context from the shift. Record the actual work delayed alongside repair and rental expenses. A queue of pallets is evidence of disruption; assigning it a cash value needs the costs your facility actually incurred.

## Use the history before the next spending decision

Nick's point is to replace a vague sense that a machine is unreliable with a record you can discuss with maintenance and finance. Look for recurring faults, repeated spending, and the work affected before deciding whether a different maintenance plan, rental cover, or replacement deserves closer evaluation.

That history also depends on operators continuing to report problems. When they raise a concern, explain who is reviewing it and what happens next. Nick warns that reports which appear to go nowhere make people less likely to speak up again.
