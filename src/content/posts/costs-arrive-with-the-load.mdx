When I was running a recycling facility, a load arrived with a huge chunk of ice in it. It was probably thousands of pounds. I wanted to reject the load or make a deduction, so I asked how we did that. Nobody knew. It had never been done.

People were concerned about recoveries, but nobody was really keeping track of how contaminated the incoming material was. Looking at that ice, I could see we were already losing thousands of pounds before we had even started processing the load.

That experience has stayed with me because the same question belongs in many operations: what are we asking our people and equipment to achieve with what we have given them? A performance discussion can spend a lot of time on what happens inside the facility while giving much less attention to the condition in which the work arrives.

## Look at what the operation was given

In that facility, I wanted incoming quality in the discussion alongside recoveries. We needed to understand the material we could reasonably expect to recover and have a way to respond when a load arrived in an unacceptable condition. My question about rejection or a deduction exposed a gap in how we dealt with that.

The distinction matters when deciding where to put effort. We may be able to improve the process. We may also be taking on unusable material before the process starts. Those are different problems, and understanding one does not remove the need to investigate the other.

## Follow the burden into the operation

For a manufacturer, imagine an incoming batch containing material that must be separated out before production. That can create inspection and sorting work as well as a loss of usable input. The production team still needs to manage its own performance, but a discussion about the batch should also cover what arrived, what could be used and whether accepting it created work the plan had not allowed for.

A 3PL might face a different version: saleable goods arrive in a presentation that requires additional hand sorting or relabelling. The goods may all remain usable. Here, the burden is the handling work. If that work is part of the agreement, it needs to be reflected in the workload and service price. If the load arrives differently from what was agreed, the team needs to record that difference and bring it into the customer conversation. A recovery percentage would tell us little about that situation.

In both cases, I would want the discussion to follow the load far enough to understand what the operation has inherited. What condition was expected? What arrived? What extra work or loss followed, and who can decide how to handle it? That gives operations something specific to discuss with the people arranging the supply or agreeing the service.

The lesson I took from the ice was to question what we were accepting as part of the job. Improving the work inside the facility remains important. So does recognizing when a load brings a cost with it, and having a practical way to address that before it becomes another burden the operation is expected to absorb.
