## Set limits around the work each load needs

In Nick's example, different truck types needed different areas and took different amounts of time. One truck stopping to complete paperwork could block the route for everyone else. A total truck count concealed those constraints.

Before setting a booking limit, identify where each load can be handled, how long it normally occupies that area, and which people and equipment it needs. Two empty dock doors offer little help if the next load needs side-loading space that is already occupied.

Nick proposed limiting bookings by truck type and directing loads to the appropriate area. That gives the team a way to spread demand across the resources doing the work.

At 09:00, both van doors are free, but the flatbed bay is occupied. At 10:00, the bay can take a flatbed while both van doors are booked. Offering the same availability to every carrier would miss that difference.

Check shared constraints as well. If those areas depend on the same operator or access lane, their combined bookings need to respect that limit. The illustration assumes separate capacity; your booking rules need to reflect how your facility actually works.

Knowing the load in advance also lets the team prepare its forms and tell the driver where to go. Nick stresses giving clear instructions about where drivers can wait or complete paperwork, so those tasks do not block the route.

The manager in Nick's story ultimately decided the facility was not ready to change. Agreeing the process with the team is part of the work: who sets the limits, how carriers book, and how exceptions get handled.
