Two clients each ship 100 pallets a week from your warehouse. The first uses the same carrier most weeks. Its dispatcher knows the order references, asks for an available window and turns up as agreed. The second client sends over a list of released orders and expects your team to arrange the collections. On Thursday afternoon its usual carrier drops out. Someone at your desk now has to get the replacement’s details, agree a new time and tell shipping which truck to expect.

Both accounts can look equally busy on the loading dock. If both pay the same per-pallet rate, the second account leaves less of that fee to cover the rest of the warehouse operation. Before deciding who gets to book their own pickups, work out which jobs the client has asked the warehouse to do. A brand that pays you to manage collections has a good reason to expect you to make that Thursday call. A brand with its own transport team may prefer to make the call itself.

## Follow one order from release to collection

A client releasing an order authorizes the warehouse to hand specified goods to the intended party. Someone then names the carrier and arranges a time that the dock can handle. Finally, staff check that the truck at the door is entitled to take that order. On an ordinary Tuesday one email can appear to cover all of this. A last-minute carrier change makes the separate responsibilities visible.

The International Association of Refrigerated Warehouses describes both the customer arranging a pickup appointment with the warehouse and the customer passing that task to its chosen carrier. Its operations guide also calls for the driver to provide the correct purchase order or release number before the warehouse releases the goods. <Cite id="1" title="Guide to Effective Warehouse Operations" citation="IARW, 2018" /> A client can give its dispatcher latitude to choose an available time while the warehouse keeps control of the handover.

Try that distinction with the people who cover the booking inbox. If a new carrier emails asking for Thursday’s 2 p.m. slot, can staff see that the client nominated it for this order? Can they offer the time immediately, or does the load need the shift lead’s approval? What will shipping check at collection? Put the answers beside the account name on the shared booking sheet. The colleague covering lunch should be able to give the same answer as the regular coordinator.

## Put the office work alongside the pallet count

For some accounts, managing the carrier is part of the attraction of buying warehousing and transport together. A respondent to *Inbound Logistics’* 2026 3PL survey described expanding its transportation management offering alongside a new facility. <Cite id="2" title="2026 Inbound Logistics Perspectives 3PL Market Research Report" citation="Gresham, 2026" /> When your commercial team sells both services, it can define how far its work extends. Sourcing a carrier, arranging a time with a client-nominated carrier and approving a carrier’s own booking are three quite different promises.

Look at a week of completed pickups for each account. Count the requests the desk approved as submitted. For the others, identify what made a person intervene. The hours spent waiting for someone to reply belong on a service timeline. The minutes the coordinator actually spends on the phone or at the keyboard belong in the staffing estimate.

A study at Schenker Logistics took this kind of account-level approach. The researchers used observations, interviews and time records to identify what drove administrative work across customers. <Cite id="3" title="Analyzing administrative processes in a 3PL company" citation="Fritzson and Dietersdóttir, 2012" /> That gives a warehouse a better starting point for a bid than assuming the office workload rises in step with pallet volume.

Before pricing the extra desk time, check what prompted the calls. If shipping kept changing the ready time, the warehouse has found a cost it can reduce itself. If the client relies on the desk to coordinate its carriers, sales has a service to describe and cost.

## Set the booking rule account by account

A client with its own transport team can nominate carriers and take responsibility for routine scheduling. The warehouse can still review exceptions that affect its ability to ship, such as a change from a pallet pickup to a hand-loaded trailer. For a client buying managed coordination, the desk can take the release, contact the nominated carrier, secure a time and send the confirmation. The client knows whom to call if the first carrier cancels.

That choice can be written into the handling rate, covered by an account fee or charged per appointment. LAX Freight Delivery’s outbound tariff, for example, lists $15 per appointment for carrier scheduling and coordination, including dock assignment and yard communication. It describes its published rates as guidelines subject to the individual agreement. <Cite id="4" title="Warehousing Rules Tariff · Rule 400" citation="LAX Freight Delivery, 2026" /> The example is useful for its scope as well as its price. The buyer can see which work the fee covers.

Once those responsibilities are agreed, a scheduling system can reflect them for each client. DataDocks has <SmartLink href="https://docs.datadocks.com/locations/current/advanced/companies/" anchorText="separate company settings" title="Companies" snippet="DataDocks Docs" /> for creating carrier accounts and automatically approving appointments. A trusted client can add the carriers it selects while the warehouse reviews booking requests. For another account, bookings can go straight into an approved state even though the warehouse retains responsibility for carrier setup. On a spreadsheet, the equivalent is to give the desk a named owner for carrier changes and a clear rule for which bookings need review.

Read the proposal once more with Thursday’s replacement carrier in mind. For the managed account, the promise might say, “For each released order, we’ll arrange the pickup window with your nominated carrier and send you confirmation. If you change carriers, we’ll contact the replacement and reconfirm the window.” For the client with a transport team, spell out which of those same calls its dispatcher owns. Sales can price the work, shipping knows whose confirmation to expect, and the person answering the phone on Thursday knows whether to make the next call.
