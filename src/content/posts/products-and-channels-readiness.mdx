Imagine a food business preparing to launch new products, add retail accounts and start selling directly to consumers. There may be a good opportunity in each. The question I would ask before committing is how much of that change the operation and its people can handle.

I would want to understand how the team responds when something unfamiliar goes wrong, what the existing operation is already dealing with, and what the additional work will cost. Those questions help distinguish an expansion we can support from one we need to prepare for first.

## Understand what each form of expansion changes

For frozen-food businesses, new customers may also change which facilities you need. Review whether your <SmartLink href="/posts/frozen-food-network-fit" anchorText="frozen-food distribution network still fits your customers" /> before committing to more storage or longer delivery routes.

Adding retail accounts and selling directly to consumers are different operating challenges. I would be careful about grouping them together as more sales and assuming the operation can absorb them in the same way. New products bring testing requirements, and changes to packaging can create work the team has not had to solve before.

For a business still getting established, I would be cautious about pursuing all those routes at once. You are asking the operation to handle several kinds of change while it is still finding its feet. It is worth being clear about which demands you are proposing to add before deciding the business is ready for the whole expansion.

An established company may have more experience and resilience to draw on. But years in business can also mean years of doing things in one familiar way. If the team has rarely had to change its processes, that history alone tells me little about how it will handle the next challenge.

Research on grocery retail illustrates the extra work a new channel can bring. In <SmartLink href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/joom.1362" title="Evolutions of Omni-Channel Fulfillment Performance: An In-Depth Case Study in Grocery Retailing" snippet="Journal of Operations Management · 2025" anchorText="their 2025 study of one UK retailer over eight years" />, Stuart Milligan and colleagues observe that “online sales create additional activities, which complicate fulfillment.” Their case concerns the retailer’s operations behind online sales. For a food business considering its own expansion, the practical question is what additional activities its particular proposal would create.

## Look at how your people respond to unfamiliar problems

I would start with the people expected to make the change work. When a process fails, do they investigate what is missing and come back with possible solutions? Or does the problem keep coming back to management with no suggestion about what to try?

Take a change in packaging size. If the existing approach does not work, I want the team asking why and exploring what else could work. Who else is out there? What have people in the industry tried successfully? There is useful work to do between identifying the difficulty and asking someone else to resolve it.

That is the kind of adaptability I would look for before adding more products or processes. Expansion will bring problems that need troubleshooting. The people doing the work need to be willing to investigate them, and the people leading them need to help that happen.

A capable plant manager and good supervisors matter here. They can motivate the wider team to take on a different process and work through the difficulties that come with it. I would want to know whether we have those people in place and how they respond when the familiar approach stops working.

## Use recent operating problems as evidence

We can learn about readiness from work the team has already had to handle. I would look at overtime and whether the operation is running one or two shifts. How much effort does it currently take to get the existing business out? That gives us a starting point for discussing additional demands.

Then I would go back to the last rush order. Could the team handle it, or did it become a major disruption? The answer tells us something about how the operation responds when the work changes unexpectedly. I would want that experience in the conversation before agreeing to take on more.

I would ask the same question about the last quality-control problem. Was the team able to deal with it? An issue occurring and a team being unable to resolve it are different things. What interests me is how the people and operation responded when they had to work through the problem.

Taken together, those experiences give us a more useful view than a general assurance that everyone will manage. If the operation is continually struggling and every unexpected request becomes a major problem, I would get that under control first. We need to understand what the current business is asking of the team before increasing the demands on it.

## Work out what the additional business will cost

<ExpansionCosts />

Once we have looked at the people and the operation, we still need to understand the economics. How much more overhead will the proposed work require? Will we need overtime? Do we need different machinery, including packaging machinery? Those costs belong in the discussion about whether to expand.

It is usually possible to find a way to make something happen. What I want to know is whether we make money doing it. If fulfilling the new business requires additional labour or equipment, we need to understand what that does to the margin before making the commitment.

A <SmartLink href="https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/product-complexity-and-operational-performance-a-systematic-liter/" title="Product complexity and operational performance: A systematic literature review" snippet="CIRP Journal of Manufacturing Science and Technology · 2019" anchorText="2019 review by Trattner and colleagues" /> found that greater product complexity was generally associated with poorer cost and time performance across the literature it examined. Its findings on quality and delivery were less clear. That gives us another reason to examine the work and expense associated with a broader offering, while assessing the consequences for our own operation.

Existing capabilities can also shape what expansion is possible. In <SmartLink href="https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/09/30/misfits-markets-ceo-discusses-the-companys-surplus-food-and-fulfillment-strategy-at-groceryshop-2026/" title="How did Misfits Market expand beyond ugly produce?" snippet="FoodNavigator · 2026" anchorText="FoodNavigator’s September 2026 reporting on Misfits Market’s move into B2B fulfilment" />, CEO Abhi Ramesh says “our most important asset at Misfits is the perishable infrastructure”. He is describing capabilities behind that company’s expansion. For our business, I would want to establish which capabilities we already have and what the proposed work would require us to add.

There may be business we deliberately take on as a loss leader. That can be a choice, provided we understand the margin and know what we are agreeing to. We should be able to explain the economics alongside our ability to do the work.

Before committing to more products or channels, I would bring those questions together: can our people adapt, what does recent operating experience tell us, and what will the additional work cost? The answers should make clear what we can take on now and what needs attention first.
