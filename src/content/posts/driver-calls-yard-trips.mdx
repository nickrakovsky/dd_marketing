Halfway through counting a mixed pallet, the receiving lead gets a call. A driver is still parked by the fence. The lead walks out, finds the cab, gives the driver the new door number and comes back to the pallet. The appointment sheet shows one truck checked in and one truck unloaded. Where would anyone looking at that sheet see the trip to the fence?

When your 3PL takes on another client, you might use last month’s truck count to estimate what the receiving team can handle. Yet forty drivers can pass through the same gate on two shifts and make very different demands on the people inside. To estimate that work on paper, count the drivers who needed an instruction, tally the repeat contacts and time how long each kind of contact occupied staff.

## Count the people involved in one instruction

Follow one driver from check-in until they know where to go. At an Italian logistics hub studied by Tea Castellucci and colleagues, a gate employee relayed the door or waiting-area instruction received by radio from staff inside. <Cite id="1" title="Transition to synchronization-driven smart inbound logistics" citation="Castellucci et al., 2024" /> To cost that exchange, count the time each warehouse employee spends on it. If two employees spend half a minute each, the instruction uses one minute of warehouse labor. A driver could then wait 20 minutes for a door while both employees go back to their other work.

Some calls take a longer route. In a 2026 study of small Polish road carriers, a participant described drivers calling the shipper, receiver or their own boss to find the right gate or contact. <Cite id="2" title="Information inaccuracy and flow disturbances in transport process" citation="Ryciuk and Kuźmicz, 2026" /> The portion that belongs on a 3PL’s labor sheet is the time its own people spend answering or following up. That distinction matters when someone suggests using driver wait times to estimate how much work the receiving desk could recover.

## Three 40-driver shifts with different workloads

Suppose the first door instruction takes one minute of staff time. All three shifts below handle 40 drivers. What changes is how often someone has to make a second call or walk into the yard, and how long that takes.

| Shift | Initial instructions | Extra contacts | Staff time per extra contact | Total warehouse labor |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Few follow-ups, short walks | 40 min | 4 | 3 min | **52 min** |
| Regular follow-ups | 40 min | 10 | 5 min | **90 min** |
| Frequent follow-ups, longer walks | 40 min | 20 | 7 min | **180 min** |

On the middle shift, the extra contacts take 50 minutes. They use more of the crew’s time than giving all 40 drivers their first instruction. What brought people back for a second conversation? If most of the ten contacts followed door changes, making the first instruction quicker would leave the largest part of the work in place.

To run the numbers yourself, add a tally beside the appointment whenever someone has to call again or go outside. Time a few of those contacts, including the return walk when the trip is solely to find the driver. If the lead was already going to that door, count the extra detour. A walk that delivers the first instruction belongs in the initial-instruction time. Keep required escorts on the sheet as work the site will still need to staff.

The tally also helps you choose what to change. If the crew must escort drivers, a closer staffed waiting point could shorten those trips. If drivers keep asking for an instruction twice, look at who can confirm the message reached them.

## Test how much work a change would remove

Take the middle shift again. The receiving lead wants to text door assignments and check the replies. Budget an extra 30 seconds for every driver, including those who would have found the right door on the first try.

## Look at the work those minutes interrupted

At Day & Ross, dock planners tell gate security where to send each arriving driver. The yard team also makes two rounds a day to check that trailers are where the records say they are, regional operations manager Jason Kirk told *Truck News*. <Cite id="4" title="Manage fleet yard traffic in name of safety, productivity" citation="Shchedrina, 2023" /> If your crew makes similar rounds, those checks still belong in the shift plan even when every driver gets clear directions at the gate.

Put a time of day and a job beside the minutes on your own sheet. If one client’s drivers account for most of the repeat contacts, put the account name beside them, too. Five walks during the last receiving hour can pull the lead away just as another client’s freight needs checking. Five walks spread across a quiet afternoon might fit between jobs. If the change lets the crew finish a paid receipt before overtime starts, the shift record and payroll can show what that was worth. If the benefit is an uninterrupted half-hour to finish a pallet count, the shift lead can point to the completed count. That is a much firmer basis for a staffing decision than multiplying every minute in the yard by an hourly wage.
